Can we pick training scenarios better than chance? The research program, explained

Three experiments in, the honest answer is: not yet — every engineered selection method sits at parity with random. But along the way the measurement itself turned out to be broken, and fixing it changed what the earlier results meant. This page tells both stories: what was tried, and how the yardstick was rebuilt so the next attempt can actually be judged.

Two colors are used consistently everywhere below: ENGINEERED — scenarios chosen or generated by the mining pipeline RANDOM — scenarios drawn uniformly at random (the chance baseline)

1 · The question, and why it's harder than it looks

In one line: the pipeline that turns real agent traces into training scenarios has one step whose value was never proven — whether choosing scenarios intelligently beats choosing them at random — and chance is a much stronger opponent than it sounds.

The repo mines ~1,000 recorded customer-service conversations ("traces") into training scenarios: a task plus a judge-gradable checklist. A fine-tune trained through this pipeline beats the base model. But the pipeline's selection step — extract behavioral facets, cluster them, pick a diverse spread — had never been tested against the null hypothesis. And the null hypothesis has a structural advantage: a uniform draw automatically matches the corpus (and therefore the eval) distribution, while any diversity-seeking selector deliberately moves away from it. Beating random requires knowing something a coin flip doesn't.

2 · What was tried: three arms, one verdict each

In one line: summary-based selection lost to random, capability-based selection recovered to exact parity, and nothing so far has been better than chance.

Arm 1 — mined v1 ENGINEERED cluster on task phrasing
Lost to random (−8.0 pts vs the multi-seed mean). Post-mortem found two mechanisms: clustering on how tasks are worded split one capability into duplicate clusters, and the diversity-seeking allocator drifted the domain mix away from the corpus (15% airline training data — a domain the eval doesn't contain).
fix: embed capability, not phrasing
Arm 2 — mined v2 ENGINEERED embed "[domain] summary | tools: signature"
Exact parity with the random mean (48.6% vs 47.4% on the final pool, paired 33W/27L/14T). Fixed the retail collapse outright. The whole facet→cluster→select machinery now buys what random.shuffle buys — at the same cost on every axis, including training-set size.
literature survey: what could actually win?
Arm 3 — generative synthesis ENGINEERED not yet built
The next bet, chosen from a 25-source verified survey: skill-extraction-driven synthesis (generate novel scenarios from a skill inventory, not replay traces) — the one approach in the literature with a teacher-controlled, budget-matched win over random selection. Difficulty-aware budget reallocation is the companion arm.

All arms share everything except selection: same scenario synthesis + validation (gpt-5.4 writes, Opus 4.8 back-checks), same self-collection (the student attempts each scenario 6× in a simulated environment; a judge keeps ≤2 passing episodes), same LoRA recipe, same eval protocol (3 passes per scenario, means only).

3 · The one trick this session actually shipped: a yardstick you can't fool

In one line: the same experiment read against one random draw said "random wins by 14 points," and read against the fair baseline says "parity" — so the fair baseline is now locked in before any new arm runs.

Same question: is mined-v2 better than random?
identical training runs, identical eval protocol — only the baseline construction differs
what do you compare against?
The naive readOne random draw, 21-scenario eval
Random = 36.5%, mined-v2 = 30.2% → "mining loses by 6." That draw turned out to be the luckiest of three seeds, on an eval pool whose noise floor (±5.5 pts) exceeded most effects being measured.
The fair read3-seed mean, 74-scenario frozen pool
Random = 47.4% ± 3.8, mined-v2 = 48.6% → parity (+1.2, inside noise). The decision rule is pre-registered: a new arm "wins" only if it clears the mean by >2× the seed spread (7.6 pts) with a paired sign-test p<0.05.

Three commitments now make the yardstick tamper-proof, all recorded in the goal charter before any generative arm exists: the eval pool is frozen (74 validated held-out scenarios, committed at 4adc4b5); the baseline is a multi-seed mean, never a single draw; and the decision rule is fixed in advance, so no post-hoc framing can turn parity into a win. Budgets are matched everywhere — scenario count, collection compute, and training-sample count (which had silently varied by up to 46% between arms).

5 · What the literature says the next arm should be

In one line: a 25-source adversarially-verified survey found exactly one approach with a controlled, budget-matched win over random selection — synthesize novel tasks from an extracted skill inventory — and explicitly demoted the ideas we liked most.

The survey (107 sub-agents, 124 extracted claims, each surviving claim confirmed by 3 independent verifiers) mapped five literatures. What survived:

  • Adapt — skill-extraction synthesis (Instruct-SkillMix): 1K synthesized examples beat 5K uniform-randomly selected real ones ~2.4×, and the win survives a teacher-controlled 1K-vs-1K ablation. This is the template for Arm 3: our facets + tool signatures already approximate the skill inventory.
  • Adapt as companion — difficulty-aware budget reallocation (DART-Math, HS-STaR): vanilla filtered collection starves hard scenarios (our dead-slot problem); reallocating rollout budget toward the ~12.5–87.5% pass-rate band wins budget-matched — but only by 1–2 points, and selection without reallocation gains +0.2%, i.e. nothing.
  • Demoted — capability composition: the same paper's ablation shows mixing two skills adds ~nothing over extracting them well. This was our favorite idea before the survey.
  • Flagged for us: difficulty gains shrink to ~1 pt on strong models because scenario diversity becomes the bottleneck — independent support for generation over selection. Three plausible-sounding claims (including "Self-Instruct beat real data") were refuted in verification and are marked do-not-cite.

6 · The unsolved design problem blocking Arm 3

In one line: every scenario so far was validated by checking its checklist against the real trace it came from — generated scenarios have no source trace, so they need a new gate before any of them may touch training.

The current validity gate is back-agreement: a judge confirms the synthesized checklist is faithful to the source conversation. A generated scenario has no source conversation. The planned replacement (designed, not yet built) has three parts: agreement against the skill specification that prompted the generation; a solvability probe inside the simulated environment; and an embedding-space duplicate check against the frozen eval pool — with the maximum similarity reported — so generation can never quietly leak eval content into training. 7 · How the results are protected

In one line: five separate artifacts tried to corrupt these numbers — a reasoning leak, two network-poisoned collections, a fake 0%, and a disk-full training crash — and every one was caught and answered by re-running, never by patching.

  • A serving flag omission silently merged the model's private reasoning into its visible replies — would have poisoned all training targets; caught by a smoke generation before collection.
  • Two collection runs were killed by network trouble (one outage, one flapping SSH tunnel whose aggressive keepalives amplified stalls into 220 connection errors). Both outputs were quarantined and both arms re-collected whole.
  • A shell quirk sent one eval a nonexistent model name → 0.0%; deleted, re-run.
  • A training crash at step 70/105 traced to checkpoint writes overflowing the nearly-full GPU box disk; fixed by disabling mid-run checkpoints (pure I/O, identical optimization).
  • Inherited from earlier debugging: the judge must see the agent's final message and needs an 8K-token verdict budget — both bugs would have deflated every arm equally and invisibly.

The blunt version: after three engineered arms and roughly 4,000 evaluated episodes, nothing beats a coin flip yet — and the program's honest output so far is a measurement apparatus rigorous enough to know that, plus a literature-vetted plan for the first attempt that plausibly could.
